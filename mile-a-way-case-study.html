Project Overview

Connecting hearts across distance

Mile-A-Way is a mobile companion app for a theoretical haptic wearable that transmits real-time touch sensations between users separated by distance.

The Vision

While video calls let us see and hear our loved ones, they leave out the most human dimension of connection — touch. Mile-A-Way imagines a world where putting on a haptic wristband or glove and opening the companion app lets you hold hands, share a comforting squeeze, or send a playful tap to someone miles away.

Our team designed and tested a companion app prototype that controls touch intensity, patterns, and privacy settings — all while integrating seamlessly into existing communication habits.

The Problem

Long-distance love lacks
one crucial sense

Technology connects our eyes and ears across the globe — but touch, the most primal form of human bonding, remains out of reach.

01

No Physical Intimacy

Video calls and messaging platforms rely entirely on audio and visual cues, making it impossible to replicate the emotional weight of physical touch.

02

Limited Emotional Expression

Subtle emotional nuances — a comforting hand on a shoulder, a reassuring squeeze — are impossible to communicate through existing digital channels.

03

Social Isolation

Long-distance relationships frequently produce feelings of loneliness and disconnection, even when communication is frequent and high-quality.

04

Therapeutic Limitations

Therapy modalities that leverage physical presence and touch-based interventions face hard limits in remote settings.

05

Accessibility Gaps

Users who are deaf, non-verbal, or have other communication differences often find voice and text-first tools inadequate for nuanced emotional expression.

06

Language Barriers

Cross-cultural, multilingual couples lose emotional nuance in translation. Touch is a universal language that transcends linguistic barriers.

Design Process

From empathy to prototype

We followed a rigorous user-centered design process, anchored in real user voices at every stage — not assumptions.

1

Discovery & Contextual Inquiry

We conducted one-on-one interviews and contextual inquiry sessions with long-distance couples and individuals in relevant demographics. We observed real communication behaviors — video calls, messaging, and current workarounds — to identify needs, frustrations, and desires.

Key Research Insights

What users actually want

Six hours of interviews and contextual inquiry sessions surfaced four definitive themes that shaped every design decision we made.

"

Emotional Nuance Over Physical Recreation

Users consistently emphasized emotional connection over literal touch replication. The ability to express feelings — not simulate physics — was the deeper desire. Conventional bonding methods were less important than meaningful, emotional signal.

"

Personal Customization is Non-Negotiable

Every user expressed a strong desire to define their own touch vocabulary — custom patterns, intensity settings, and rhythm preferences. No two relationships communicate the same way, and the app needed to reflect that.

"

Privacy is a First-Class Feature

Across every participant, privacy and control were paramount. Users needed to control who can initiate touch, what types of touch are permitted, and how their interaction data is used and stored. Trust must be earned before intimacy is shared.

"

Seamless Platform Integration

Users didn't want yet another standalone app. The haptic experience needed to integrate naturally into existing video calls and messaging workflows — meeting them where they already were, not asking them to change their habits.

Evaluation Findings

Testing revealed what mattered most

Three rounds of evaluation — formative, heuristic, and summative — generated concrete findings that shaped every iteration of the design.

Heuristic Evaluation

Form fields lacked proper labels; carousels created accessibility issues. Touch controls needed consolidation — 3 clear, well-labeled options outperform many confusing ones.

Formative Testing

Onboarding screens were flagged as redundant. The "Welcome to Mile-A-Way" screen provided more value than standalone onboarding flows — users wanted to get to the experience faster.

Usability Testing

Users successfully completed core tasks but requested more contextual feedback during haptic sessions. Visual indicators of "touch received" were identified as a critical addition.

Privacy Insights

Privacy settings surfaced as the most scrutinized section — users needed complete confidence in the consent model before they would feel comfortable using the device in personal contexts.

Integration Insight

Participants consistently asked whether Mile-A-Way would "just work" inside FaceTime or WhatsApp. Platform integration emerged as the single biggest adoption driver.

Customization Validation

Custom touch pattern creation was the most positively received feature across all sessions. Users spent more time in customization than any other area of the prototype.

Reflection

What this project taught us

Learnings from the full design cycle

From initial proposal to final usability test, this project was a full-cycle lesson in human-centered design under real-world constraints.

User-centered design changes everything

Engaging directly with potential users revealed needs we never would have discovered on our own. The shift from "what would be cool" to "what do users actually need" was the single most impactful move we made — and it happened because we listened first.

Emotional safety in research requires intentional design

Given the intimate nature of our subject matter, we learned to design the research environment as carefully as we designed the product. Having one interviewer per session, using careful language, and creating genuine psychological safety was as important as the interview guide itself.

Privacy is a design feature, not an afterthought

Every evaluation phase reinforced the same message: trust is the prerequisite for intimacy. Building consent and privacy controls into the core experience — not as a settings screen buried in a menu — was a non-negotiable design principle.

Iteration is not failure — it's the process

The formative evaluation exposed real problems with our initial wireframes. Instead of being discouraging, reviewing the work through fresh eyes before advancing gave us the exact information we needed to design something worth testing. The system worked.

Next Steps

Future iterations would focus on: deeper platform integration APIs, refined haptic feedback vocabulary (expanded pattern library), enhanced session feedback indicators, and accessibility auditing for users with motor differences. The core concept validated — the product is worth building.
