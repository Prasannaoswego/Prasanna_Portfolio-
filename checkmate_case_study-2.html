01 — Overview

What we built, and why.

CheckMate is a streamlined web-based task management application designed for everyday people — not power users, not IT teams. Just humans who need to stay organized.

The brief was deceptively simple: build a to-do list app. But every product designer knows that "simple" hides layers of decisions. Who is the user? What's the minimum viable clarity? How do you avoid building another Trello that intimidates more than it helps?

This case study walks through how I led the requirements and usability workstreams — connecting stakeholder intent to design decisions, and design decisions to a testable, shippable product — across 7 biweekly Agile sprints with a cross-functional team of 7, including IBM industry professionals.

The design principle that guided everything

"Simplified and clean with more options available when needed — not features piled up, but intentional layers."

— Direct client quote, Interview 1

03 — Discovery

The client said "simple." We asked what that meant.

"Monday is too busy. Trello is cool but too simple. I want something clean — but not basic. Nail down the base features. Don't let it become a monster."

— Client, Stakeholder Interview 1

This single quote became our north star. Before a single wireframe was drawn, I ran 5+ structured stakeholder interviews — with a deliberate strategy: ask about pain, not features. The questions were organized by domain (Engine, GUI, Usability, QA) to ensure every team had the signal they needed to make decisions without coming back for clarification.

Interview Finding 01
Users want priority and due dates — not labels
When asked about organization, users consistently described urgency ("I need to remember this is due tomorrow") rather than taxonomy. This shifted our priority model from tags-first to deadline + priority-first.
Interview Finding 02
Sharing is wanted — but not the primary flow
Clients wanted task sharing ("with my family") but not as a core feature. This helped us scope: build the sharing infrastructure, but don't design the UI around it. Sharing is a power feature, not the entry point.
Interview Finding 03
Calendar is a stretch goal — integrate, don't duplicate
Users wanted calendar integration but understood it adds complexity. Client explicitly asked: "Don't build a calendar inside the app if it means breaking the simplicity." This became a documented scope boundary in the SRS.

Requirements Decision
Scope the "shared lists" feature without killing it
The client wanted sharing but signaled it wasn't high priority. Rather than deprioritizing it entirely, I documented it as an in-scope feature with a reduced implementation footprint — build the "Shared With Me" page and sharing infrastructure, but don't design complex permission models. This preserved the feature while protecting the team's velocity.
Result: Sharing shipped. Permission complexity didn't slow us down.
Documentation Decision
Two user manuals — because documentation is a design artifact
We produced two versions of the User Manual: an early-sprint version for internal reference and a final polished version for delivery. I treated the manual not as an afterthought but as a design handoff artifact — a reflection of the interface's clarity. If something was hard to document, that was a signal the UI needed rethinking.
Result: The manual doubled as a UX audit tool.

Phase 01 — Paper Wireframes

The messy, essential first step.

Before touching Figma, we went analog. Paper wireframes were the team's shared brainstorming space — fast, low-stakes, and deliberately rough. The goal wasn't perfection; it was to externalize competing ideas quickly enough to argue about them in the room.

My contribution at this stage was less about drawing layouts and more about translating requirements into spatial decisions — making sure the components we'd committed to in the SRS actually had a logical home on the page.

Key questions we were wrestling with on paper:

Q1 Should task details be hidden by tabs, revealed by click, or always-visible in a panel?
Q2 Pop-ups or a persistent side panel for task creation and editing?
Q3 How should the overall content hierarchy be framed — what's primary, what's secondary?

Phase 02 — Digital Wireframes

Structure meets placement.

Moving to Figma, the three-panel layout that emerged from paper became the foundational grid of the application. My role here was ensuring the layout honored the requirements I'd written — every SRS-defined component needed a visible home.

I was conscious of two competing risks: designing something so generic it felt like Todoist, or so unique that familiar patterns were lost. We studied Trello and Todoist deliberately — not to copy them, but to understand where users' mental models lived, and build from there.

MY DESIGN CONTRIBUTION AT THIS STAGE
→ Defined persistent left panel with My Tasks, Recently Deleted, My Projects, Shared With Me hierarchy
→ Decided right panel should be persistent (not a popup) — users shouldn't lose context to edit a task
→ Specified checkbox + title + due date + priority as the minimum viable task row — no more, no less
→ Pushed for "Create New Task" button prominence after noting its discoverability risk in requirements review
"The team remained cognizant of existing to-do list designs (Trello, Todoist) while maintaining a unique visual identity — a good frame of reference for standard element placement and association."

Phase 03 — Low-Fidelity Prototype

First time the design breathed.

The lo-fi prototype was the first version of CheckMate you could actually use — interactive checkboxes, hover states, basic page transitions. For the first time, users could walk through a real flow instead of imagining it from static frames.

The defined user flow for testing: Login → Create Project → Add Task → Edit Description → Delete Task. Five steps that covered the entire core value loop of the product.

FEATURES INTRODUCED FOR USER TESTING
+ Interactive checkboxes with state change
+ Hover states on task rows and buttons
+ Completed tasks view (separate from active)
+ Restore from completed — ability to bring tasks back
+ Priority selector (Low / Medium / High) with visual differentiation
DESIGN DECISION AT THIS STAGE

Time limitations meant we couldn't test every feature we'd prototyped. Rather than test a diluted version of everything, I advocated for scoping the test to the core user flow only — login through delete. Better to get rich signal on 5 tasks than shallow data on 15.

Phase 04 — High-Fidelity Prototype

Full fidelity. Real design system. Stakeholder-ready.

The high-fidelity prototype incorporated everything learned from testing — and made several hard scope calls. Here's what changed from lo-fi, and why each decision was made.

REMOVED
Subtasks & Tags

These existed in the lo-fi. Usability data and time pressure both pointed the same direction — they added cognitive overhead without adding value for our "everyday person" target user. Removing them was a team decision I championed based on what I was hearing in testing sessions.

Result: Cleaner task creation. Faster time-to-first-task.
ADDED
Delete Confirmation Popup

Multiple users in testing expressed anxiety about accidental deletion. This wasn't a feature request — it was an observed emotion. The confirmation popup was a direct response to that fear. Good design answers feelings, not just use cases.

Result: Users explicitly praised this in post-test feedback.
ENHANCED
Interactivity & Animations

Page transitions, hover state polish, panel slide animations, and checkbox completion micro-interactions were all added in the hi-fi pass. These aren't decorative — they provide feedback, communicate state, and make the app feel intentional rather than assembled.

Result: Product felt "finished" — not just functional.

06 — Design Decisions

Every choice had a reason.

Spanning requirements, research, and design gave me a rare vantage point — I could trace every pixel back to a user insight or a documented requirement. These are the decisions that defined CheckMate's character.

These are the decisions that shaped the product.

Design Stack
Figma Wireframes + Prototype
FigJam Paper wireframe ideation
Miro Affinity diagrams + sprint board
WebAIM Contrast ratio validation
IA Decision
One task per view. Multiple lists, separate pages.
The client was explicit: one list per page, but the ability to navigate to others. This became the foundational IA decision — a persistent left panel for navigation between "Projects," a main content area for the active list, and a right panel for task details. Nothing competes for attention. Everything has a lane.
Aligns with "simplified and clean" client mandate. Prevents cognitive overload.
Navigation Decision
Three-panel layout: stable, contextual, and focused
Left panel: persistent navigation (My Tasks, Recently Deleted, My Projects, Shared With Me). Center: active list. Right: task details panel. This pattern mirrors productivity tools users already know (Notion, Todoist) while maintaining our own visual identity — reducing learning curve without looking derivative.
Reduced navigation errors in usability testing vs. earlier iterations.
Scope Decision
Removed subtasks and tags in high-fidelity
The low-fidelity prototype included subtasks and tags. Usability testing and time constraints revealed they added complexity without adding clarity for our target user ("Mom, Dad, everyday person"). I led the decision to remove them from the high-fidelity prototype — a hard call that sharpened the product. Features exist to serve users, not to impress reviewers.
Cleaner task creation flow. Faster time-to-first-task for new users.
Interaction Decision
Delete confirmation popup + soft-delete recovery
Deleting a task is irreversible in most apps. We added two layers: a confirmation popup on delete, and a "Recently Deleted" page with task recovery. This was born from a usability interview insight — users frequently expressed anxiety about losing tasks by mistake. The design directly answers an observed fear, not an assumed one.
Users explicitly praised this in post-test interviews. Zero accidental data loss.
07 — Accessibility

WCAG 2.1 wasn't a checkbox. It was a constraint we designed within.

🎨

Color & Contrast

Every text-background pairing validated using WebAIM. Designed to pass WCAG AAA — not just AA. The client mentioned considering "eyesight and distractions" in interview 1; this became a design constraint, not a recommendation.

WebAIM validated · WCAG AAA target
🔤

Typography: Inter at 14pt

Selected Inter typeface: sans-serif, tall x-height, highly legible at small sizes. Set minimum text size to 14pt — 2 points above the WCAG 12pt requirement. A deliberate buffer, not the minimum bar. Spacing and line-height optimized for comfortable reading, not maximum density.

Inter · 14pt minimum · Optimized line-height
👆

Signifiers & Interaction

Multiple signifier types used consistently: icons denote element types, buttons use contrasting colors with hover states, proximity groups related items. Keyboard navigation designed in from the start. Labels are descriptive, not decorative — every interactive element communicates its function.

Icons · Hover states · Keyboard nav · ARIA labels
08 — Research & Usability Testing

We tested what we built, not what we imagined.

Planned a formal IRB study with 11 participants. Adapted to pragmatic informal testing due to time constraints — without compromising on the rigor of what we observed.

Interview Protocol — 3 Phases
Qualitative

Focus: To-do list habits, pain points, workarounds
Usability tasks: Create account → Create list → Modify/delete/recover tasks
Post-test: Ease rating (1–10), challenges, improvements

Quantitative

What the Think-Aloud Sessions Revealed
Synthesis · Affinity Mapping
Finding A
"Create project" and task deletion buttons weren't prominent enough. Users looked in wrong places.
Finding B
Users confused "projects" vs. "tasks" hierarchy. The mental model needed clearer spatial separation.
Finding C
Users expected inline task editing directly in list view. The side panel wasn't discoverable enough.
Finding D
Calendar/date picker behavior broke workflow. Users expected click-out to dismiss, not a separate close action.
09 — Usability Pain Points

What we fixed, and what we documented for next.

01
Project Management Discoverability
Create project and task deletion buttons were not prominent. Users scanned the interface without finding them. Fix: Increased visual weight of primary action buttons. Added "Create New Task" consistently in both the homepage and project views.
02
Projects vs. Tasks Hierarchy Confusion
Users unsure whether to create a "Project" first or a "Task" first. Fix: Added inline instructional text on first-time views: "Create a project first, then add tasks to it." Progressive disclosure instead of expecting users to figure out the hierarchy on their own.
03
Task Editing — Missing Signifiers
Users expected to click a task and edit it directly in the list. The right-panel interaction wasn't surfacing correctly. Fix: Added edit icon to the task details panel header, added hover highlight on tasks to signal interactivity. Both solutions logged as sprint backlog items.
04
Menu & Calendar Dismiss Behavior
Calendar date picker didn't dismiss on outside click — a standard interaction pattern users expected. Menus didn't always close intuitively. Fix: Implemented click-outside dismiss for all floating elements. Documented as a QA test case for all future builds.

13 — Lessons

What we'd do differently.

"
The most important skill in a cross-functional team isn't your craft. It's knowing when to speak, when to listen, and how to translate between languages — between what designers say and what engineers hear, between what clients ask for and what users need.
Requirements · Usability · Design Lead
Direct, concise requirements prevent redundant meetings. Verbosity in documentation is a cost paid by every person who reads it — write for the reader, not the reviewer.
Requirements Lesson
Cross-team communication should be scheduled, not assumed. We committed to at least one joint meeting per sprint. When we kept that commitment, velocity improved. When we didn't, surprises happened at demo time.
Collaboration Lesson
Plan the IRB study earlier. We had the rigor but not the runway. An 11-participant formal study would have given us richer data — we adapted well, but adapted less is still less.
Research Lesson
QA integration shouldn't wait for stable builds. Shifting test planning left — earlier in the sprint — means bugs found earlier are bugs fixed cheaper. We learned this the hard way in Sprint 5.
Process Lesson
14 — What's Next

Version 1 is the beginning.

01
Additional Social Logins
GitHub SSO shipped. Apple and Google sign-in are the next authentication paths — reaching users who don't have GitHub accounts (which is most of our target "everyday person" audience).
02
Task Duplication, Repetition & Search
Power users with many tasks need duplication (copy a task), recurring tasks, and a search bar. These features were intentionally scoped out of v1 to protect simplicity — they're the right v2 priority.
03
Automatic Database Migration
As the schema evolves, automated migration prevents manual intervention during updates. A technical next step that enables faster design iteration without breaking existing user data.
