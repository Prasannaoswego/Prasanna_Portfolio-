01
The Research Gap

Most studies evaluate apps in isolation. This one puts them head-to-head.

Food delivery platforms serve millions of users daily, yet there is limited structured, side-by-side comparative usability research evaluating them under identical conditions. I identified an opportunity to design a controlled study where the same 15 standardized tasks were executed by the same participants across both platforms. By holding variables constant, I allowed the data — not assumptions — to reveal the differences.

🟢

Uber Eats

Larger ecosystem. Promotes well. Clean layout. But the search function? A glaring blind spot that users discovered in real-time.

🔴

DoorDash

Superior search accuracy and category organization. But cart management and promotional clarity consistently frustrated participants.

Despite being such a huge delivery service app, it failed at something as simple as search. I searched 'Indian' and it showed me nothing related. I'd rate Uber Eats a 1 out of 5 for search.

02 — The Methodology

Designed to
eliminate bias,
surface truth.

I did not simply conduct usability sessions; I deliberately architected a study framework designed to be both scientifically valid and practically revealing. Every methodological decision — from task design to participant selection — was structured to reduce bias, ensure consistency, and produce measurable, comparable outcomes across platforms.

Design Choice
Within-Subject Design

Every participant tested both apps. This eliminates performance variability between individuals — the same person, same conditions, two platforms. Direct comparison.

Design Choice
Randomized Task Order

Which app comes first was randomized per participant. This controls for learning effects — you can't credit familiarity for speed improvements.

Design Choice
Mixed Methods

Quantitative (SUS, task time, error rate) + Qualitative (think-aloud, observational notes, post-task interview). Numbers tell what. Stories tell why.

Design Choice
Local Context

All participants from Syracuse, NY. National studies mask local behavior — here, limited cuisine availability in mid-size cities directly affects satisfaction and search.

Design Choice
IRB-Approved Ethics

Full IRB protocol. Anonymous participant IDs (P1–P6). No payment flows completed. Zero deception. Data stored password-protected, deleted after 6 months.

Design Choice
Think-Aloud Protocol

Participants verbalized every thought in real time. This surfaces cognitive friction that metrics alone can't capture — hesitation, second-guessing, confusion.

Study Flow
Step 01

Recruitment & Screening

16-question screener to filter participants: 18+, food delivery app users within past 6 months, residents of Syracuse NY, comfortable with technology. Gender-balanced 50/50. Age range 23–52.

Step 02

Informed Consent

Full IRB-compliant consent process. Participants briefed on purpose, recording, anonymity, and voluntary withdrawal rights. Sessions conducted over Zoom with screen-share.

Step 03

15 Standardized Tasks

Moderator read verbatim scripts. No guidance unless explicitly asked (recorded as error). Tasks spanned onboarding, search, navigation, cart modification, promotions, reordering, and icon recognition.

Step 04

Post-Task SUS + Interview

10-item System Usability Scale (Brooke, 1986) on a 3-point Likert scale per app. Followed by 10-question semi-structured interview probing satisfaction, pain points, visual design, and preference.

Step 05

Triangulated Analysis

Quantitative: descriptive statistics on task time, success rate, error rate. Qualitative: content analysis + thematic coding of think-aloud transcripts and interview responses. Narrative analysis of individual user journeys.

Step 06

Debriefing

Every participant received a formal debriefing document explaining study goals and confirming data protection. Researcher contact provided for any post-session questions.

Researcher Thinking

The task script was read verbatim — not summarized, not paraphrased. This deliberate constraint ensures the moderator's tone doesn't prime the participant toward success or frustration. Consistency is validity.

The Paradox

Uber Eats won on promotions, cart management, reordering, icon clarity, and customer support. Yet DoorDash had the higher overall SUS. Why? Because search is a foundational function — and Uber Eats failed it completely. One deep failure can sink a constellation of strengths.

07 — Key Findings

What the
data revealed.

🔍
DoorDash Wins
Search is DoorDash's Superpower

Every participant who searched for cuisine types (Indian, Italian) found DoorDash dramatically more accurate. DoorDash also showed open vs. closed status, relevant dish variants, and linked to direct items from search. Uber Eats showed irrelevant results or nothing at all.

🛒
Uber Eats Wins
Cart & Reorder UX is Uber's Edge

All 6 participants scored Uber Eats 3/3 for cart management. The "Order Again" tab was praised as highly convenient. DoorDash's cart editing was described as "complicated" and required more steps — small friction that accumulates over daily use.

📢
Uber Eats Wins
Promotion Design Matters More Than Quantity

Uber Eats showed ONE highlighted promotion clearly. DoorDash showed many scrollable offers with food images participants found distracting. The research reveals a core UX principle: clarity beats abundance in promotional design.

🎨
Uber Eats Wins
Color Psychology is Undefeated

Unprompted, participants described DoorDash's red as "flaky" and "less trustworthy." Uber Eats' green was associated with savings, calm, and reliability. Color wasn't just aesthetic — it shaped trust and brand perception before a single feature was tested.

📦
DoorDash Wins
Non-Food UX — DoorDash Thinks Like a Marketplace

For toilet paper and grocery searches, DoorDash surfaced the items directly with price and details — no extra store-navigation step needed. Uber Eats forced users into a two-step search (store → search within store). For convenience users, this friction is critical.

💡
The Paradox
More Wins ≠ Higher SUS Score

Uber Eats won more individual feature categories, yet DoorDash scored 10 points higher overall on SUS. The lesson: foundational failures (like search) create disproportionate negative impressions. One critical failure can overwhelm many individual wins.

08 — Design Recommendations

Evidence-based.
Actionable.
Specific.

🟢 Fix for Uber Eats

01
Rebuild the search algorithm — urgently Searching "Indian" or "Italian" must return relevant restaurants and dishes. The current failure is catastrophic for the core use case. Implement cuisine-tagging and NLP-based menu search. This is P0.
02
Distinguish restaurants from convenience stores in search results Participants were confused when pancake searches returned stores with frozen food. Add visual tags like "Restaurant" vs. "Store" labels directly in search results.
03
Make open/closed store status unmissable DoorDash blurs closed stores. Uber Eats buries the "available next day" text. Apply visual de-emphasis (blur, opacity) on unavailable stores consistently.
04
Surface promotion relevance in the "Offers" tab The bottom nav Offers tab felt unnecessary to users. Either integrate promos contextually at checkout or remove the tab and surface deals within category browsing.

🔴 Fix for DoorDash

01
Simplify cart management Quantity changes and item removal should be achievable in 2 taps or less. Currently requires more steps than Uber Eats. Swipe-to-remove and inline quantity steppers are proven patterns.
02
Redesign promotion presentation Showing many offers simultaneously dilutes attention. Test a single hero promotion with secondary browsable deals, rather than a flat carousel. Reduce food imagery in deal cards — it distracts from the offer.
03
Make reordering more visible on the home screen Previous orders should surface as a prominent section on the homepage, not buried in navigation. "Order again" is a high-frequency use case — give it first-class real estate.
04
Simplify profile editing and account settings Participants found phone number editing in DoorDash "twice as complicated" as Uber Eats. Audit the account settings IA — simplify with a single-screen edit flow.
09 — Why This Study Is Different

Not another
opinion survey.

Differentiator
Verbatim Task Script

Most studies improvise task instructions. Here, every word was pre-written and read aloud identically across all 6 sessions. This removes moderator bias from the equation entirely.

Differentiator
Local User Context

Testing in Syracuse, NY revealed locale-specific issues: Uber Eats' search failures were amplified because Indian cuisine availability in mid-size cities is limited — every missed result mattered more.

Differentiator
The SUS Paradox Finding

The revelation that Uber Eats won more features but scored 10 SUS points lower is a publishable finding — it demonstrates that weighting of core tasks in user perception doesn't correlate linearly with feature wins.

Differentiator
IRB-Level Rigor

Full ethical protocol, CITI training completion, anonymized participant IDs, zero deception, informed consent, voluntary withdrawal. This isn't a class exercise — it meets publication standards.

Differentiator
Triangulated Methods

Three analysis methods running in parallel: content coding of think-aloud transcripts, thematic analysis of interview responses, and narrative analysis of individual journeys. Each validates the others.

Differentiator
Real Participants, Real Friction

Zero simulated behavior. Every quote, every hesitation, every "I don't understand why" moment was observed live and documented. The data has the texture of real experience.

The Takeaway
Design is a
series of bets
on what users need.

DoorDash bet on search. Uber Eats bet on visual clarity and convenience loops. Both bets paid off — in different ways, for different users. The job of a UX researcher isn't to declare a winner. It's to make the bets more informed.

The goal wasn't to pick a winner. It was to understand why users feel confident in one place and frustrated in another — and build a bridge between those two experiences.
